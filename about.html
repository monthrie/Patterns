One cord, bouncing.

The story behind a small web tool, an elderly Portuguese craftsman, and a kind of textile that — as far as we can tell — nobody else on Earth makes.

An 18×10 board. Watch: the cord starts at the dashed ring, travels at 45°, bounces off the edges like a billiard ball, and returns exactly to where it began. Then a second cord covers everything the first one missed. When the last cord closes, the weave tightens into finished cloth.

The craft

João hammers nails around the edge of a board, ties a cotton cord to one, and pulls it diagonally across. At each edge it catches a nail and bounces. It crosses other strands — over, under, over, under — until eventually it arrives back at its starting nail, where he splices the end to the beginning so cleanly you cannot find the join.

The result is real cloth with no warp, no weft, no hems and no stitches. Every strand in it is a closed loop. He has made cushions, bags, shoes (12 loops) and an entire jacket — exactly 8 loops of cord, ~200 hours, not one seam. If you pulled a strand free and shook it out, you'd be holding one enormous unbroken circle.

The painstaking problem

Here's the catch: João doesn't design the pattern. He only designs the board's shape — the rule does the rest. And the one thing he needs to know before hammering a hundred nails is: how many separate cords will this shape need?

For years the only way to find out was to trace the whole path by hand on paper, bounce by bounce — hours per attempt, one slip and the trace is worthless. In practice he could never explore beyond four loops. The shape of every future piece was rationed by pencil labour.

The mathematics

It turns out this question has a deep history. The bouncing paths are mirror curves — the same mathematics behind the sona sand drawings of Angola, Celtic knotwork and Tamil kolam. For a plain rectangle there is a theorem: an m×n board needs exactly gcd(m, n) cords. 18×10 → 2. 17×10 → 1. 15×10 → 5. The cloth computes the greatest common divisor of its own sides.

But for the shapes João actually builds — L-shapes, crosses, boards with holes — no formula is known. Counting the loops of an arbitrary shape without tracing is an open problem, posed as recently as 2020. The only general tool is simulation: run the billiard, see what closes.

So this app does exactly that. Draw any shape; it traces every path in milliseconds, counts the cords, colours them, measures how many metres of each colour to buy. The wall that capped João at four loops is gone — he has since designed and physically woven 15-loop patterns that quite possibly no human had ever seen, since they exist only by computation and the space of shapes is astronomically large.

A principle to play with

Two cords is two cords. As long as a rectangle's sides share a greatest common divisor of 2, the weave has the same character — the same cloth at different sizes:

18×10, 14×12, 8×6 — all gcd 2, all the same two-cord cloth.

And some boards are special: when the sides share no divisor at all, a single cord covers the entire board and returns home — the hardest kind to weave (one pass drags the whole cord's length through every crossing) and the kind João once insisted was the only kind worth making, before he discovered that extra loops are extra colours:

17×10 — gcd(17,10) = 1. One cord. No visible beginning, no end.
