**Subject:** Woven mirror curves: a craft submission, and a question about scope

Dear Interlace editors,

I am writing partly to ask whether the journal is open for submissions yet, and partly because I think we may have something genuinely unusual for you, and I would value your steer on how best to frame it.

A retired Portuguese craftsman, João, weaves textiles in which every strand is a single closed 45 degree billiard path on a nail board: the cord bounces off the boundary, crosses itself over and under by checkerboard parity, and returns to its own start, where he splices it invisibly. There are no cut ends, no warp, no weft, no seams. Each finished piece is, in the strict sense, a physical alternating link. He has made a wearable jacket this way (probably four components, a count even he cannot verify), and shoes that are a single 12 component object. He invented the whole method himself, knowing nothing of *sona*, mirror curves, Gerdes or Jablan.

These are the mirror curves of Chokwe sand drawing, Tamil kolam and Celtic interlace. As far as we have been able to find, they have always been drawn, never woven. We believe this may be the first documented practice of weaving them as textile.

The reason I think it fits Interlace rather than a general craft venue is that the mathematics is not decoration on top, it is the medium. A few threads we can show:

1. Boards realize gcd in cloth: an m by n rectangle needs exactly gcd(m, n) strands, with equal lengths governed by lcm. A two colour board comes out as two interlocking loops every time, the same characteristic pattern recurring across his whole black and white body of work.
2. Loop number on irregular boards is an open problem (no closed form). Using a billiard simulator I built for João, we computed it for every polyomino up to 15 cells (3.4 million shapes), validated it against the gcd theorem and the known scaling law, and found regularities that appear to be unrecorded, including an integer sequence not in the OEIS.
3. Joining two flat 8 strand panels into a 3D shoe yields 12 strands. That fusion is link surgery performed by hand, and our simulator cannot yet predict it. A craft operation is doing mathematics the software cannot reach.

My questions are simply: is the journal accepting submissions, and on what timeline? And given the range above, which aspect would be most valuable to you, and at what length? I can see this as a full theoretical article, a shorter technique piece, or even ephemera (the OEIS sequence alone). I would rather write the version you actually want than guess.

I have attached five photographs: João wearing the jacket; the app's billiard trace beside the same board woven in cotton; his hands interlacing cord with a needle over the parity sheet he designed; two pairs of his shoes, each a 12 strand object built from two flat 8 strand panels; and a two colour piece in which the two closed loops, one black and one white, always resolve into the same characteristic interlocking pattern. The physical pieces exist and are wearable and exhibitable, and João is open to commissions and to teaching. If any of this resonates, I would also welcome any pointers to people or venues in the mathematical fiber arts world who might want to see it in person.

With thanks, and admiration for what you are starting,
