## Step 0 — A shape in the head

It begins with an intention, not a pattern. A waistcoat. A letter of the alphabet. A board that will carry fifteen colours. João decides what he wants and, crucially, *what colours he wants*, before he knows what pattern the shape will give him. The pattern is not his to choose. Only the boundary is.

> **[PHOTO]** `studio/20260224-joao-portrait-outdoors.jpg` (João among the trees) or `studio/20260602-joao-portrait.jpg` (at his bench under the spools).
> **[QUOTE]** "It's something invented. Nobody taught me. And I still have the fun and the thrill of catching new stuff to do." (`20260602-1943-interview` [04:02])

## Step 1 — Planning: counting the loops before a single nail (THE ONE STEP THE TOOL CHANGED)

This is the only place the computer enters the story, and the page should say so plainly. For most of his working life João planned on paper: he drew the whole bouncing path by hand on graph paper, bounce by bounce, and numbered the nails to count the separate circuits, Arabic numerals for the first circuit, Roman for the second, the rest left blank. Hours of pencil work for a single guess, and one slip ruined the trace. He could never see past four loops.

Now he draws the shape on a phone and the answer comes in seconds: how many cords, what colours, how many metres of each. That is the whole of what the tool does. It did not make the cloth easier to weave. It made the pattern possible to *see* before a hundred hours are committed.

## Step 2 — The paper

Before any string, the board is dressed with a sheet of his own design: a basket-weave grid where small circles mark where the nails go and small dashes say, at every crossing, over or under. It is a lookup table for the whole weave, printed once and read a thousand times. He used to draw each sheet by hand and photocopy it; now it prints at any size.

> **[PHOTO]** `paper/20260407-basketweave-paper-board-nails.jpg` (the paper taped to a board, no string yet) and the macro `stills/d-paper-glance.jpg` (his finger on the dashes).
> **[QUOTE]** "Over, under, over, under. It's written there." (`20260218-craft-demo-paper` [00:00])

## Step 3 — The nails

He hammers a nail at every boundary mark, through the paper, into the board. A bare grid of nails standing in an empty rectangle, the cloth not yet begun.

## Step 4 — The first cord, all the way round

He ties a cord to a nail and pulls it across at forty-five degrees. It catches a nail, bounces, crosses the board again, bounces again, and keeps going. A single cord does not cover a little area and stop. It travels the *entire* circuit, dozens or hundreds of bounces, until it arrives back at the exact nail where it began. Only then is one loop laid. At every crossing he glances at the paper: over, or under.

## Step 5 — Closing the loop, invisibly

When the cord returns home, he does not tie a visible knot. He splices the end back to its own beginning with small pieces cut from the same string, sewn with a needle, so finely that the join cannot be found. This is what makes each cord a true closed loop, and the whole piece a thing with no beginning and no end.

> **[QUOTE]** "It always joins to itself. I cut the same strings, and this I use with needles, so you won't notice it. It's easy." (`20260218-interview-booklet-circuits` [02:48])

## Step 6 — The next cord, and the next

A pattern with five loops needs five separate cords, each its own colour, each laid the same painstaking way, each closing on itself. The board fills colour by colour until every crossing is covered.

> **[PHOTO]** the sequence continues: `process/20260408-seq02-diamond-field.jpg` → `seq03-five-colours.jpg` → `seq04-nearly-covered.jpg` → `seq05-full-density-on-nails.jpg`.
> Here is the counter-intuitive truth the maths page proves and this page should state once: *fewer* loops is *harder*, not easier. One cord covering a whole board has to be dragged, in full, through every crossing. Many short loops are quicker handwork. The simplest-looking pieces cost the most labour.
> **[QUOTE]** "There's less walking around with the material from one side to the other. That means the job can be done easier." (`20260218-craft-demo-paper` [01:04])

## Step 7 — Building the body (the passes)

The cloth is not one thin layer. To give it body, each cord is run through its path more than once. In the thick wool pieces, a cord is traced three times over, so what began as a sparse net of single strands becomes dense, full cloth.
> ⚠ VERIFY with João: is the 3-pass densify universal, or specific to the thick wool / 3D pieces? The jacket is documented as 3mm cord filling a 9mm gap (three passes). Flat cotton mats may be a single pass. State precisely once confirmed.

> **[QUOTE]** "You want them to be enough to fulfil the three layers... twelve big pieces or eight big pieces around. You've got to be focused, so you won't get to a point that's helpless." (`20260218-craft-demo-paper` [01:28])

## Step 8 — Off the nails: it becomes cloth

When every cord is laid and every pass done, he lifts the weave off the nails. Freed, it tightens and shrinks into finished fabric, the loose net pulling into a dense, self-supporting cloth. He trims the few tails. There are no hems to sew, because no thread ever ended at an edge.

> **[PHOTO]** the sequence finishes: `process/20260408-seq06-off-nails-loose.jpg` → `seq07-tightened.jpg` → `seq08-texture-detail.jpg` → `seq09-finished-cross-mat.jpg`.
> **[QUOTE]** "They always shrink. Because you tighten them up." (`letters-alphabet-stories` [01:34])

## Step 9 — When it is not flat: the third dimension

A mat is one board. A shoe or a jacket is not. For those, the whole process above is only the beginning. He weaves the parts flat as a string *skeleton*, ties them together into the three-dimensional object using the cord itself, and only then lays the colour, following the path through a garment that already exists in space, in the air, in his hands. It cannot be done on a table. It is the hardest thing he does.

## The close — the number

End the page on time. Not the mathematics, not the tool: the hours. Over a hundred for a waistcoat. More than two hundred for the jacket, made overnight across many days, by hand, every loop laid and closed and laid again. State it plainly and let it land.

> **[QUOTE]** "If I say more than 100 hours, I don't know if it's more than 200... I never did something more complicated than this." (`20260603-jacket-presentation` [03:05])
